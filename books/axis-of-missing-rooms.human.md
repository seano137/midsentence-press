## A Note on the Older Book

This novel begins in conversation with Flatland: A Romance of Many Dimensions by Edwin Abbott Abbott, first published in 1884.

The image carried forward is the arrival of the Sphere in the Square’s world. To the Square, a three-dimensional visitor can only appear as a changing two-dimensional cross-section: a circle that grows and diminishes, without the Square being able to see the whole object responsible for it.

That image raises a question I want this story to pursue: what if the thing we cannot perceive is not far away, or hidden behind a wall, but immediately adjacent to us? What if the limits of our perception are also the limits of our maps, our architecture, our memories, and our ideas of what it means for someone to be missing?

The characters, setting, and events that follow are original.

## Chapter One
### The Survey

The first thing Mara Venn noticed about the hotel was that its shadow fell in the wrong direction.

She stood across the road with a paper cup of coffee cooling between her hands, watching the late-November light settle over the roof. The sun was low behind the western ridge. Every other building on the street cast its shadow eastwards, long and thin across the pavement.

The hotel cast its shadow towards the sea.

Mara blinked, moved three steps to her left, and looked again.

The shadow had not moved.

She took a photograph.

Then she looked at the building itself.

The Meridian Hotel had been closed for eleven years. Its pale stone frontage was streaked black beneath the windows, and its ground-floor entrance had been boarded over with sheets of plywood. A faded sign still hung above the door. Only the letters MERI remained clearly visible. The rest had been eaten by rust.

It was a narrow building, five storeys high, squeezed between a pharmacy and an empty shop that had once sold musical instruments. Its proportions were ordinary enough. The roofline was slightly crooked, the windows too tall, the brickwork repaired in patches. A building surveyor could have described it in a dozen unremarkable sentences.

Mara had been paid to describe it in several hundred.

She checked the address on her work order.

Meridian Hotel, 17 West Quay, Bellwether.

She checked the address painted on the temporary demolition notice.

17 West Quay.

There was no mistake.

She crossed the road.

A temporary fence surrounded the building. Behind it, weeds had grown through cracks in the forecourt. A contractor’s sign announced that demolition would begin on Monday, subject to final structural clearance.

Mara read the date.

Monday was in four days.

She took another photograph.

Her phone vibrated.

SANA IDRIS:

You arrived?

Mara typed back.

Outside.

The reply came almost immediately.

Don’t go in alone.

Mara looked up at the hotel.

I’m a surveyor, Sana. Not a burglar.

You know what I mean.

Mara put the phone away.

She did know.

She had been avoiding Bellwether for nineteen years. Not continuously, and not with any great drama. She had simply made choices that took her elsewhere. University in Bristol. A first job in Leeds. A second in Rotterdam. A flat in London. A career that involved being sent to buildings with problems, inspecting them, writing reports, and leaving before the repairs were finished.

She had become very good at leaving buildings.

Bellwether was the place she had left behind.

The Meridian was the reason.

She walked to the gate.

A brass padlock hung from the chain, and beside it was a laminated card with a telephone number for the site manager. Mara called. A man answered after five rings, sounding as though he had been interrupted halfway through a sandwich.

“Construction.”

“Mara Venn. I’ve been appointed to complete the pre-demolition structural survey at the Meridian.”

A pause.

“You’re the engineer?”

“Surveyor.”

“Right. You’re early.”

“The order says access from today.”

“Nobody told me.”

“Then somebody should have.”

The man sighed.

“Give me ten minutes.”

He hung up.

Mara stood beneath the sign and waited.

A gull landed on the hotel roof. It looked down at her, considered something, and flew away.

She took out her notebook and began recording observations.

External masonry: weathering consistent with age and exposure.

Roofline: apparent settlement to rear elevation.

Window frames: timber, probable water ingress.

Shadow: anomalous orientation.

She stopped.

She looked at the last line.

Then crossed it out.

There were perfectly ordinary reasons for shadows to behave strangely. Reflected light. An unseen structure. A sloping surface. A cloud passing over one building but not another. She had spent too much time looking at old buildings to mistake a momentary optical oddity for evidence of anything.

Still, she checked the photograph.

The shadow was there.

She enlarged the image. The hotel stood in the centre of the frame, the street around it grey and flat. Its shadow stretched across the road towards the harbour.

Mara turned the phone sideways.

The shadow remained.

She saved the photograph and put the phone away.

Ten minutes later, a white van pulled up outside the fence.

The driver was a short, broad man in a fluorescent jacket. He climbed out carrying a ring of keys.

“Mara Venn?”

“Yes.”

“Peter Hales. Site manager.”

They shook hands.

Hales looked at the hotel, then at her.

“Bit of a dump, isn’t it?”

“That’s why I’m here.”

He gave a brief laugh.

“Fair enough. You’ve got a hard hat?”

“Yes.”

“Boots?”

She lifted one foot.

“Good. Watch the rear stairs. We had a ceiling come down last month.”

“Was anyone injured?”

“No. Nobody’s been in there for years.”

He unlocked the gate.

Mara followed him across the forecourt.

“How long has it been empty?”

“Eleven years, officially. Longer, if you count the upper floors.”

“Why?”

“Water damage. Then the owners went bust. Then there was a legal dispute. You know how it is.”

“I know how buildings become empty. I asked why this one did.”

Hales looked at her.

“Because it was falling apart.”

“Was that the original reason?”

“Does it matter?”

“It might.”

He unlocked the plywood door and pulled it open.

A smell came out.

Dust, wet plaster, old carpet, and something faintly medicinal.

Mara stood on the threshold.

The reception hall was dark. A long counter occupied the far wall, its polished surface dulled beneath a film of grey dust. Behind it, a set of pigeonholes had been left open. A brass bell sat on the counter beside a register swollen by damp.

The lobby chandelier was still hanging.

Three of its glass drops were missing.

“Power’s off,” Hales said. “I’ve got a torch.”

“I have one.”

“Right. I’ll show you the safe route through the ground floor. After that, you’re on your own.”

Mara switched on her torch.

The beam crossed the lobby and caught a framed photograph on the wall.

The Meridian Hotel, 1958.

A group of people stood outside in summer clothes. Men in dark suits, women in bright dresses, children holding ice creams. The hotel behind them looked new and proud. The sign above the door was complete.

MERIDIAN.

Mara stepped closer.

At the edge of the photograph, almost outside the frame, stood a boy.

He was thin, with dark hair and a pale shirt buttoned to the neck. His face was turned towards the camera, but his eyes seemed to be looking slightly beyond it.

Mara knew him.

She had seen the photograph before.

She had seen it in the newspaper, in the police station, in the folder her mother had kept inside a kitchen drawer for almost two decades.

Her brother was standing in the photograph.

Daniel Venn.

He had been sixteen when he disappeared.

Mara looked at the date printed beneath the frame.

She leaned closer.

The boy in the photograph was Daniel.

Not someone who resembled him. Not a coincidence of posture or haircut.

Daniel.

His face was the same face she remembered from the summer he vanished: the narrow nose, the uneven eyebrows, the small scar beside his mouth from falling off a bicycle at thirteen.

Mara took a step back.

“What’s this?”

Hales turned.

“What?”

She pointed at the photograph.

“Where did this come from?”

“Been here since I started.”

“When did you start?”

“Ten years ago.”

“Who put it up?”

“Don’t know.”

Mara looked at the date again.

“That photograph is from 1958.”

“Looks like it.”

“My brother is in it.”

Hales stared at her.

“Your brother?”

“Daniel Venn.”

He glanced at the photograph, then at her.

“You’re from here?”

“Yes.”

“Didn’t know.”

“He’s in that picture.”

Hales approached the wall.

He took the photograph down and examined its back.

“Maybe it’s been misdated.”

“No.”

“Could be a relative.”

“No.”

“Look, I don’t know what to tell you.”

Mara took the frame from him.

The glass was cold.

She examined the paper around the edges. It had yellowed. There was a faint watermark in the lower corner. The photograph itself was black and white, with the grain and contrast of a mid-century print.

It was not a recent imitation.

She turned it over.

A handwritten note had been fixed to the back with old adhesive tape.

Meridian summer gathering, August 1958. Staff and guests.

Below it, in a different hand:

Daniel, please stop standing at the edge.

Mara felt her stomach tighten.

Her brother had always stood at the edge of photographs.

He disliked having his picture taken. He used to move behind people, or stand just outside the frame, or turn his face away at the last moment. Their mother had once joked that Daniel was trying to become invisible.

Mara had laughed.

She was twelve.

Daniel had disappeared the following year.

“Can I take this?” she asked.

Hales frowned.

“Probably not.”

“Why?”

“Belongs to the building.”

“My brother is in it.”

“Then perhaps you should speak to the owners.”

“The owners are demolishing the building.”

“Which is why we’re here.”

Mara looked at him.

“Who has the archive?”

“What archive?”

“Photographs. Records. Guest books. Anything that was left.”

“There’s a cupboard in the office. Most of it’s ruined.”

“Where?”

Hales pointed towards the back.

“Through there. But you can’t spend all day looking at old papers. I’ve got contractors coming Monday.”

Mara put the frame carefully on the counter.

“I’ll need to document it.”

“Fine.”

He handed her the keys.

“Just don’t fall through anything.”

Then he walked back towards the entrance.

“Where are you going?” Mara asked.

“Outside. Phone call.”

He left her in the lobby.

The door closed behind him.

Mara stood beneath the chandelier, holding her torch.

For nineteen years, she had imagined Daniel’s disappearance in a thousand different ways.

He had run away.

He had drowned.

He had been taken.

He had decided that the family was no longer worth returning to.

He had been frightened of something.

He had been angry with her.

The police had found no evidence of foul play. No body. No confirmed sighting after the evening he vanished. The case had gradually become a file that was reviewed once a year, then once every few years, then only when her mother asked.

Her mother had died six years ago.

Mara had kept the file.

She had not opened it for three.

She looked at the photograph again.

Daniel had been standing outside the Meridian Hotel in 1958.

He had been born in 1982.

She took a picture of the picture.

Then she put the frame back on the counter and walked towards the office.

The corridor behind reception was narrow and poorly lit. A series of doors led off it. The first was marked STAFF. The second, MANAGER. The third had no label.

The office smelled of damp paper.

A metal filing cabinet stood against one wall. Its drawers had been left open, their contents scattered across the floor. An old desk occupied the centre of the room. A telephone sat on it, its cord cut.

Mara began with the filing cabinet.

The top drawer contained invoices from the final years of operation. The second held staff rotas. The third was jammed.

She pulled harder.

The drawer came free with a metallic shriek.

Inside were dozens of envelopes, a collection of keys, and a small black notebook.

The notebook was dry.

Mara opened it.

The first pages contained lists of room numbers and maintenance notes.

Room 104: tap leak.

Room 207: broken latch.

Room 312: replacement bulb.

The handwriting was neat and slightly slanted.

She turned several pages.

The dates were from 1997.

Then she found a page with a heading.

ROOMS NOT ON PLAN

Beneath it were five entries.

0A.

0B.

0C.

0D.

0E.

Beside each was a date.

The first was 14 March 1997.

The last was 22 November 1997.

Mara frowned.

There was no zero floor in the Meridian.

She knew the building’s plans. She had received them from the council before arriving. Five floors, a basement, and a roof space. The room numbers followed a conventional arrangement.

She turned the page.

A single sentence had been written beneath the list.

Do not use the north stairwell when the lift is moving.

Mara read it twice.

The north stairwell was on the building’s original plan, but it had been sealed during the 1970s. The only public staircase was at the rear, beside the lift shaft.

She closed the notebook.

Then she heard a sound.

A knock.

Three slow taps.

Mara looked towards the office door.

“Hello?”

Nothing.

She stood.

The sound came again.

Three taps.

Not from the door.

From somewhere behind the wall.

Mara approached it.

The wall was covered in old wallpaper, patterned with small blue flowers. There was a patch near the skirting board where the paper had peeled away. She pressed her palm against the plaster.

Cold.

She tapped once.

The sound came back from inside.

Hollow.

She tapped again.

A second sound answered.

Three taps.

Mara stepped away.

“Peter?”

No reply.

She took out her phone and checked for signal.

One bar.

She moved towards the doorway.

The sound came again.

Three taps.

Then a voice.

“Mara?”

She stopped.

It was a man’s voice.

Young.

Familiar.

A voice she had not heard in nineteen years.

“Mara, is that you?”

She turned towards the wall.

For a moment, she could not move.

Then she said the only thing she could think of.

“Daniel?”

There was a pause.

A small sound, like someone breathing against a microphone.

Then:

“You’re late.”

Mara put one hand against the wall.

“Where are you?”

“Here.”

“Where is here?”

A pause.

“I don’t know.”

She heard movement on the other side. Something scraping against the plaster.

“Daniel, can you hear me?”

“Yes.”

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Where have you been?”

The answer came so quickly that it frightened her.

“Here.”

Mara swallowed.

“Daniel, listen to me. I’m going to get help.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because they’ll close it.”

“Close what?”

“The door.”

She looked at the wall.

There was no door.

Only blue flowers, damp plaster, and a small crack running from the ceiling to the skirting board.

“Daniel, I need you to tell me where you are.”

He did not answer.

“Daniel?”

A long silence.

Then, quietly:

“Are you still on the other side?”

Mara felt the room tilt.

“What other side?”

“The wall.”

She stepped closer.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

His voice seemed to move away.

“Stay there.”

“Daniel, don’t go.”

“I’m not going.”

The sound of scraping stopped.

Mara pressed her ear to the wall.

She could hear something.

Not a voice.

Not movement.

A low, continuous vibration, like a distant engine.

Then Daniel spoke again.

“Mara?”

“Yes?”

“Don’t let them knock it down.”

The vibration grew louder.

“Daniel—”

There was a sudden crack.

A line appeared in the wallpaper.

It ran vertically from the ceiling, then bent sharply to the right, forming a corner that should not have been possible in the flat surface of the wall.

Mara stepped backwards.

The crack widened.

The wallpaper pulled away from the plaster, but not as paper should. It seemed to fold inward, as though the wall behind it had become a curtain.

For a fraction of a second, she saw a strip of something beyond.

A room.

A narrow room with a green carpet and a wooden chair.

A boy stood in the centre.

He was sixteen.

He was wearing a pale shirt.

His hair was dark.

His face was Daniel’s.

He looked directly at her.

Then the wall closed.

The crack vanished.

The wallpaper lay flat.

Mara stood in the office, unable to breathe.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from Sana.

I’m outside. Where are you?

Mara looked at the screen.

She typed:

I found him.

She deleted it.

Typed again:

Come inside. Now.

Then she looked at the wall.

Nothing moved.

She reached out and touched the wallpaper.

It was warm.

For a long time, she stood there with her hand against it.

Then she remembered the photograph.

Daniel at the edge of the frame.

The notebook.

Rooms not on plan.

The north stairwell.

And the voice that had said:

Are you still on the other side?

Mara took out her phone and photographed the wall.

The image showed a plain surface covered in blue flowers.

No crack.

No corner.

No room.

No boy.

She took another photograph.

The same.

A third.

The same.

She lowered the phone.

On the desk behind her, the black notebook lay open.

She had not left it open.

She was certain of that.

The page with the list of missing rooms had turned over by itself.

There was a new line beneath the final entry.

The handwriting was the same.

23 November 2016.

Mara stared at the date.

It was today’s date.

Below it, someone had written:

ROOM 0F: OCCUPIED.

Then, in smaller letters:

ONE PERSON OUTSIDE. ONE PERSON INSIDE.

Mara heard the front door open.

“Mara?”

Sana’s voice came from the lobby.

“Mara, where are you?”

Mara did not answer.

She was looking at the notebook.

A final sentence had appeared at the bottom of the page.

There are two ways to leave a room.

And beneath it:

Only one of them brings you back.

## Chapter Two
### A Door in the Wrong Place

#### 1. The Person Outside

“Mara?”

Sana Idris stood in the lobby with one hand on the door and the other holding a paper bag.

She had brought sandwiches.

She had also brought a crowbar.

Mara looked at the crowbar first.

“What’s that for?”

Sana glanced down.

“Getting you out.”

“I’m not trapped.”

“That’s what people say immediately before I have to get them out.”

“I’m fine.”

Sana put the paper bag on the reception counter.

“You look terrible.”

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

Sana was thirty-four, three years younger than Mara, and had the irritating habit of appearing entirely composed in situations where everyone else was coming apart. Her hair was tied back, her coat was buttoned to the throat, and her expression suggested that she had already considered several possible disasters and was waiting to discover which one they were dealing with.

She had been Mara’s closest friend since university. They had met during a surveying placement in a half-demolished textile mill, where Sana had spent an afternoon explaining why a load-bearing wall was not an appropriate place to attach a noticeboard.

They had been friends ever since.

Sana looked at the wall.

“Is that it?”

Mara nodded.

“The wall?”

“Yes.”

“What happened?”

“I heard Daniel.”

Sana’s expression changed.

It was slight, but Mara noticed.

“What do you mean?”

“His voice.”

“On the phone?”

“No.”

“Through the wall?”

“Yes.”

Sana put down the crowbar.

“All right.”

Mara stared at her.

“That’s all?”

“What do you want me to say?”

“I don’t know. That I’m losing my mind. That there’s a reasonable explanation. That you don’t believe me.”

“I believe that you heard something.”

“That’s not the same as believing me.”

“No. It isn’t.”

Sana stepped closer to the wall.

“Show me.”

Mara pointed to the place where the crack had appeared.

“There.”

Sana examined the wallpaper.

“Nothing.”

“I know.”

“Was there a visible opening?”

“For a few seconds.”

“How large?”

“About the width of a door. Maybe less.”

“Did you see through it?”

“Yes.”

“What did you see?”

“A room.”

“What kind of room?”

“Green carpet. Wooden chair. Bare walls.”

“Anything else?”

“Daniel.”

Sana looked at her.

“Your brother?”

“Yes.”

“How old?”

“Sixteen.”

Sana glanced back at the wall.

“Mara.”

“I know.”

“Daniel would be thirty-four now.”

“I know how old my brother would be.”

“Then we need to be careful.”

Mara folded her arms.

“Careful about what?”

“About deciding what you saw before we know what happened.”

“I know what I saw.”

“You saw something. That’s what we know.”

Mara turned away.

The distinction was reasonable.

It was also unbearable.

She walked to the desk and picked up the black notebook.

“Look at this.”

Sana read the page.

“Rooms not on plan.”

“Yes.”

“Zero A through zero E.”

“Yes.”

“And this is today’s date?”

“Yes.”

Sana examined the handwriting.

“Same hand?”

“I think so.”

“Have you seen it before?”

“No.”

“Could someone have added it?”

“Not while I was watching.”

Sana looked at the wall again.

“Who else is in the building?”

“Peter Hales. Outside when I heard Daniel.”

“Anyone else?”

“Not that I know of.”

Sana moved towards the office door.

“Let’s find him.”

“Why?”

“Because if someone is playing a trick, he might know about it. If someone is hiding in the building, he might know about that too. And if neither is true, we need to establish what else could be happening.”

Mara stayed where she was.

Sana stopped.

“What?”

“He said not to let them knock it down.”

“Who?”

“Daniel.”

Sana’s face softened.

“All right.”

“They’re demolishing it on Monday.”

“I know.”

“He said they’ll close it.”

Sana glanced at the notebook.

“Then perhaps we should make sure we understand what ‘it’ is before Monday.”

She picked up the crowbar.

Mara stared at her.

“What are you doing with that?”

“Still trying to get you out.”

“I’m standing right here.”

“Good. Then you can help.”

They walked into the lobby.

The photograph was still on the reception counter.

Sana picked it up.

She examined the date, the handwriting, and the boy at the edge.

Then she looked at Mara.

“That’s Daniel.”

“Yes.”

“I remember him.”

“I know.”

Sana turned the photograph over.

“Have you told anyone?”

“No.”

“Your mother?”

Mara said nothing.

Sana put the frame down.

“Sorry.”

“She died six years ago.”

“I know.”

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then Sana looked towards the entrance.

“Let’s find Hales.”

#### 2. The Site Manager

Peter Hales was standing beside his van, talking on the phone.

He ended the call when he saw them.

“Everything all right?”

“No,” Mara said.

Hales looked at Sana.

“Who’s this?”

“Sana Idris. I’m helping with the survey.”

Hales glanced at the crowbar.

“You’re not on the contractor’s list.”

“I’m not a contractor.”

“Then what are you?”

“Concerned.”

He looked at Mara.

“What’s happened?”

“We need to see the original building plans.”

“You’ve got them.”

“I need the complete set.”

“That’s what you’ve got.”

“No. I need the original architectural drawings, including the basement and service areas. I also need the records from the hotel office.”

Hales rubbed his forehead.

“Why?”

“Because there’s a room in this building that isn’t on the plans.”

He looked at the crowbar again.

“Is this a joke?”

“No.”

“Have you found an undocumented space?”

“Possibly.”

“Where?”

“Behind the manager’s office.”

Hales shook his head.

“There’s no space behind the manager’s office.”

“That’s what I thought.”

He stared at her.

“Look, the building’s been inspected. We’ve got structural reports, asbestos reports, fire reports. Every square metre has been accounted for.”

“Then it should be easy to prove.”

Hales looked towards the hotel.

He seemed to consider refusing.

Instead, he took the keys from his pocket.

“Show me.”

They returned to the office.

Hales examined the wall.

“That?”

“Yes.”

He knocked against it.

The sound was solid.

He knocked again.

Still solid.

“Plasterboard over brick,” he said. “Probably an old service recess.”

“There’s no recess on the plans.”

“Plans aren’t always perfect.”

“Then let’s find out.”

Hales took a small measuring tape from his jacket.

He measured the wall from the corner to the doorframe, then the opposite side of the room.

“Four metres and twelve.”

Mara checked the architectural drawing on her tablet.

“Three metres and seventy.”

“What’s that?”

“The room is forty-two centimetres wider than the plan.”

Hales looked at the wall.

“Could be the original measurement was wrong.”

“Then the opposite wall should be displaced by the same amount.”

She checked again.

“It isn’t.”

Sana stepped into the centre of the office.

“How thick is the wall?”

Hales tapped it.

“About twenty centimetres.”

“Could there be a cavity?”

“Not one that size.”

“How do you know?”

“Because I’d have found it when we did the initial inspection.”

Mara looked at him.

“When was that?”

“Three years ago.”

“Did you inspect this room?”

“Yes.”

“Did anything unusual happen?”

Hales gave a short laugh.

“Other than the roof leaking?”

“Anything.”

He hesitated.

“Why?”

“Because I think someone was in the wall.”

Hales stopped smiling.

“Someone?”

“My brother.”

The silence that followed was not disbelief.

It was recognition.

Mara saw it in his face.

“You know something,” she said.

Hales looked away.

“No.”

“Peter.”

“I don’t know anything.”

“You recognised what I said.”

“I didn’t.”

“You did.”

Hales put the measuring tape back in his pocket.

“Listen. I’ve been managing this site for ten years. People have stories about this place. That’s all.”

“What stories?”

“Old hotel. Missing people. Strange noises. You know how it is.”

“Tell me.”

He glanced at Sana.

Then back at Mara.

“Your brother disappeared here, didn’t he?”

Mara felt the air leave her lungs.

“How do you know that?”

Hales said nothing.

“How do you know?”

He walked towards the door.

“There’s a woman who used to work here. She comes by sometimes. I think she’s still in town. If you want answers, speak to her.”

“Who?”

“Elsie Ward.”

“Where does she live?”

“Old care home on the hill. Bellwether House. She won’t be difficult to find.”

Mara moved towards him.

“Why didn’t you tell me this before?”

“Because I didn’t know you were Daniel’s sister.”

“You knew about him.”

“I knew the name.”

“Why?”

Hales looked at the notebook on the desk.

“Because his name is in the maintenance records.”

Mara stared at him.

“Which records?”

“The old ones.”

“Where?”

“Basement.”

“Show me.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I don’t have access to the basement.”

He took a step backwards.

“Nobody does.”

#### 3. The Basement

The basement entrance was behind the kitchen.

It was concealed by a metal door painted the same colour as the wall. A sign had once been fixed to it, but only the word STAFF remained.

Hales led them there reluctantly.

“I’ll show you the door,” he said. “That’s all.”

“Why is it locked?” Sana asked.

“Structural risk.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the answer on the paperwork.”

Mara examined the lock.

It was an old mortice lock, scratched around the keyhole. Someone had tried to force it at some point, but the door itself appeared undamaged.

“Do you have a key?”

“No.”

“Who does?”

“Nobody.”

“Then how did you get down there during the inspection?”

“We didn’t.”

Mara turned.

“You didn’t inspect the basement?”

“The previous contractor did.”

“Where’s their report?”

“With the owners.”

“Can we have it?”

“Ask them.”

Sana crouched beside the door.

There was a strip of paper caught beneath it.

She pulled it free.

It was yellow and brittle, folded into a narrow rectangle.

On it was a number.

0F

Beneath that, in pencil:

NORTH SIDE. THIRD LANDING.

Mara looked at Sana.

“What’s that?”

Sana handed it over.

Hales took one look and stepped back.

“Where did you get that?”

“Under the door.”

“Put it back.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s not yours.”

“It’s a piece of paper.”

“Put it back.”

Mara held it up.

“What’s the third landing?”

Hales shook his head.

“There’s no third landing.”

“On which staircase?”

“The north staircase.”

“The one sealed in the seventies?”

“Yes.”

“Why was it sealed?”

“Fire regulations.”

“That doesn’t explain the paper.”

Hales looked towards the kitchen.

“I’ve got work to do.”

He left.

Sana watched him go.

“He’s frightened.”

“Yes.”

“Of what?”

Mara looked at the basement door.

“I don’t know.”

She took out her phone and photographed the note.

Then she photographed the lock.

Then the door.

She had begun to understand that photographs were useful only when they could be trusted. She wanted a record of every detail, even if the record failed to capture what she had seen.

Sana stood.

“Let’s go upstairs.”

“Why?”

“Because if there’s a third landing, it’s not in the basement.”

Mara looked at the note.

“North side.”

They walked back through the kitchen and into the main corridor.

The north staircase was behind a door at the end of the ground-floor hall.

Unlike the other doors in the hotel, this one had no handle.

A steel plate had been bolted over the frame. The paint was newer than the surrounding wall, but the screws were old and rusted.

Sana touched the plate.

“Someone wanted this sealed properly.”

Mara examined the surrounding masonry.

“Not just sealed. Built over.”

“Can we get through?”

“Not without equipment.”

Sana looked at the crowbar.

Mara gave her a look.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You’re judging me.”

“I’m considering whether you’re about to damage a building that is scheduled for demolition.”

“Good. At least one of us is thinking about the future.”

Mara took a torch and shone it along the edge of the steel plate.

There was a narrow gap at the bottom.

A draught came through it.

It was cold.

And it smelled of the sea.

Sana crouched.

“That’s strange.”

“What?”

“Air movement.”

“Ventilation.”

“From a sealed stairwell?”

Mara lowered the torch.

A faint sound came from the other side.

Footsteps.

Slow.

Measured.

Someone walking down a flight of stairs.

Sana stood up.

They listened.

One step.

Another.

Another.

Then a voice.

A woman’s voice.

“Who’s there?”

Mara moved closer.

“Hello?”

“Who’s there?”

Mara looked at Sana.

“Elsie?”

There was a pause.

Then:

“Not yet.”

Sana frowned.

“What?”

The voice came again.

“Not yet.”

Mara put her hand against the steel plate.

“Who is this?”

A soft tapping followed.

Three times.

Then nothing.

#### 4. Elsie Ward

Bellwether House stood on a hill overlooking the harbour.

It had once been a school, then a private nursing home, and now operated as a residential care facility for older people who needed assistance but did not require full-time hospital care.

Mara and Sana arrived shortly after four.

The receptionist was a young man named Oliver who wore a blue cardigan and had a small tattoo of a lighthouse behind his left ear.

“Elsie Ward?” he said.

“Yes.”

“Are you family?”

“No. We knew her through the Meridian Hotel.”

Oliver checked a list.

“She doesn’t usually take visitors.”

“Could you ask her?”

“She’s resting.”

Mara took out the photograph.

“Could you show her this?”

Oliver looked at it.

His expression changed.

“Where did you get that?”

“At the hotel.”

He looked again.

Then he picked up the telephone.

“Wait here.”

He disappeared through a door behind the reception desk.

Sana leaned towards Mara.

“Everybody knows more than they’re saying.”

“Yes.”

“That’s usually a bad sign.”

“Usually.”

They waited.

A clock ticked on the wall.

Somewhere in the building, a television played a daytime quiz show.

After several minutes, Oliver returned.

“She’ll see you.”

He led them down a corridor to a small room overlooking the gardens.

An elderly woman sat beside the window.

She was very thin, with white hair cut close to her head. A blanket lay over her knees. Her hands were folded on top of it, and a pair of spectacles rested on the table beside her.

She looked at Mara.

Then at the photograph.

“Where did you find that?”

“Reception.”

Elsie nodded.

“I thought they’d have taken it down by now.”

“Who put it there?”

“I did.”

Mara sat down.

“Why?”

Elsie looked towards the window.

“Because it was the last photograph I had of him.”

“Of Daniel?”

“Yes.”

Sana pulled a chair beside Mara.

Elsie studied them both.

“You’re his sister.”

“Yes.”

“You’re older.”

“Yes.”

“I remember you. You used to wait outside the kitchen. Your mother would send you down with sandwiches.”

Mara felt something loosen in her chest.

“You remember me?”

“Of course.”

Elsie looked at the photograph.

“He liked you.”

Mara looked down.

“Where is he?”

Elsie did not answer.

“Please.”

The old woman closed her eyes.

“I don’t know.”

“But you knew he disappeared.”

“Yes.”

“You knew something happened.”

Elsie nodded.

“Tell me.”

She opened her eyes.

“I was working nights. Reception, mostly. The hotel was full that summer. Tourists, fishermen, people coming through on the ferry. Your brother used to come in after school. He’d sit in the lobby and draw.”

“Draw what?”

“Rooms.”

Mara looked at her.

“What kind of rooms?”

“Empty ones. Doors. Staircases. Corridors that went nowhere.”

Sana took out a notebook.

“Did he ever show you the drawings?”

“Yes.”

“Do you still have them?”

Elsie shook her head.

“He gave them to me once. I kept them in a drawer. Then the hotel closed, and the owners cleared everything.”

“Why was he here?”

“He liked the building.”

“Why?”

Elsie smiled faintly.

“He said it was bigger on the inside.”

Mara felt her hands tighten.

“Did he say that often?”

“All the time.”

“Did you believe him?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I was sensible.”

The old woman gave a small laugh.

It faded quickly.

“He told me there were rooms behind the rooms. He said the building had a second set of corridors that ran alongside the ones we used.”

Mara looked at Sana.

“Did he show you?”

“No. He said I wouldn’t be able to see them.”

“Why?”

Elsie turned towards her.

“Because I was looking in the wrong direction.”

Mara said nothing.

Elsie continued.

“He used to talk about a place he called the Between. He said it was a space beside ours, not above or below it. He said the hotel was built across both.”

Sana’s pen stopped moving.

“Did he explain how?”

“Not in a way I understood.”

“Did he say he could enter it?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

Elsie looked at the photograph.

“The night he disappeared.”

Mara leaned forward.

“What happened?”

Elsie took a breath.

“I was at reception. It was just after midnight. Daniel came down from the third floor. He was carrying a notebook. He looked frightened.”

“Did he say why?”

“He said someone had followed him.”

“Who?”

“He didn’t know.”

“Did you see anyone?”

“No.”

“What happened next?”

Elsie looked at the window.

“He asked me to open the north stairwell.”

Mara felt a chill.

“The sealed one?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“He said there was a door on the third landing.”

“There is no third landing.”

“I know that now.”

“What do you mean?”

Elsie looked at her.

“I opened it.”

Sana leaned forward.

“How?”

“With a key.”

“Where did you get the key?”

“From the desk.”

Mara stared.

“Peter said nobody had access.”

“Peter wasn’t here then.”

“Who had access?”

“The manager. Me. Daniel.”

“Why Daniel?”

Elsie looked down at her hands.

“He had made a key.”

Mara felt the room shift around her.

“How?”

“I don’t know. He said the lock was wrong. He said it wasn’t really a lock.”

Sana wrote something down.

“What did you see when you opened the stairwell?”

Elsie was silent for a long time.

Then she said:

“A staircase.”

“Going up?”

“Yes.”

“How many steps?”

“I don’t know. More than there should have been.”

“Did you go up?”

“No.”

“Did Daniel?”

Elsie nodded.

“Yes.”

Mara gripped the edge of her chair.

“Did he come back?”

“Not that night.”

“Not ever?”

Elsie looked at her.

“He came back once.”

Mara stopped breathing.

“When?”

“Three weeks later.”

“Where?”

“Reception.”

“Was he still sixteen?”

Elsie nodded.

“Did he look different?”

“No.”

“Did he say where he’d been?”

“He said he’d been trying to find the way home.”

Mara stared at her.

“Then why didn’t he come home?”

Elsie’s eyes filled.

“Because he couldn’t.”

The room was silent.

Mara felt a pressure behind her ribs.

“Why?”

Elsie looked at her.

“Because when he came back, he wasn’t alone.”

#### 5. The Other Visitor

“Who was with him?”

Elsie shook her head.

“I didn’t see anyone.”

“You just said he wasn’t alone.”

“I heard someone.”

“Where?”

“Behind him.”

“Behind him where?”

“In the stairwell.”

Mara looked at Sana.

Sana’s face had gone pale.

“Did Daniel say who it was?” she asked.

Elsie nodded.

“He called it the Listener.”

Mara frowned.

“The Listener?”

“He said it was something that could hear us through the walls.”

“Was it human?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did it speak?”

Elsie looked down.

“Not in words.”

Mara sat back.

“Why didn’t you tell the police?”

“I did.”

“What did they say?”

“They said I was confused. They said Daniel had probably run away. They said the hotel was old and full of noises.”

“And you believed them?”

Elsie looked at her.

“No.”

Mara swallowed.

“Why didn’t you tell my mother?”

“I did.”

“What?”

“I went to your house.”

“When?”

“Two days after he disappeared.”

Mara stared.

“She never told me.”

“She asked me not to come back.”

“Why?”

Elsie looked towards the window.

“Because I told her Daniel had come home.”

Mara felt the room tilt.

“She knew?”

“Yes.”

“She knew he was alive?”

“She knew he had come back to the hotel.”

“Why didn’t she tell me?”

“Because she was frightened.”

“Of what?”

Elsie turned back.

“Of losing him again.”

Mara stood.

“That’s not an answer.”

“No,” Elsie said. “It isn’t.”

Sana rose too.

“Mara.”

“No. I want to know.”

Elsie reached for the table.

Her hand trembled.

“Your mother came to the hotel that night. She went into the stairwell. She saw the door.”

Mara looked at her.

“What door?”

“The one on the third landing.”

“Did she go through it?”

Elsie nodded.

“Yes.”

Mara stepped backwards.

“She went inside?”

“Yes.”

“Did she find Daniel?”

Elsie closed her eyes.

“She found something.”

“What?”

Elsie opened them.

“A room.”

“What was in it?”

“A chair. A table. A window.”

Mara stared.

“Was Daniel there?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

Elsie looked at her.

“She said she could see him through the window.”

Mara’s voice was almost inaudible.

“Where?”

“In the lobby.”

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“I know.”

“She was in a room, looking through a window, at the lobby?”

“Yes.”

“Which lobby?”

Elsie shook her head.

“I don’t know.”

Mara put one hand over her mouth.

Elsie continued.

“Your mother said she could see herself standing at reception. She could see the front door. She could see the clock. She could see everything exactly as it had been on the night Daniel disappeared.”

Mara lowered her hand.

“Was it a reflection?”

“No.”

“How do you know?”

“Because she saw something else.”

“What?”

Elsie looked directly at her.

“She saw herself walking out of the hotel.”

Mara was silent.

“She said she had never done that. She said she had stayed. She said she had searched for Daniel until morning.”

Elsie paused.

“But in the room, she could see herself leave.”

“Was it another version of her?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did she speak to it?”

“She said she tried.”

“What happened?”

Elsie’s fingers tightened around the edge of the table.

“The other woman turned around.”

“And?”

“She looked directly at your mother.”

Mara waited.

Elsie whispered:

“Then she smiled.”

The room seemed to grow colder.

“Why was that frightening?” Sana asked.

Elsie looked at her.

“Because your mother wasn’t smiling.”

#### 6. The Rules of the Between

They left Bellwether House shortly before five.

Mara had asked Elsie a dozen more questions, but the answers had become increasingly fragmented. The old woman was tired. Her memory was uncertain. Sometimes she contradicted herself. Sometimes she remembered details with extraordinary precision.

She had given them one useful thing.

A key.

It was small, made of dark metal, and shaped unlike any ordinary hotel key. It had a flat circular head with a narrow cut through the centre.

Elsie had kept it in a tin beneath her bed.

“Your mother left it with me,” she had said.

Mara had held it carefully.

“Why?”

“She told me to give it to you if you ever came back.”

“Why didn’t you give it to me before?”

“Because you never asked.”

Mara had no answer.

Outside, the light was fading.

Sana unlocked the car.

“You’re not going back there tonight.”

Mara looked at her.

“Yes, I am.”

“No.”

“Sana—”

“We’ve got a locked staircase, an undocumented room, a key from a woman who may be remembering things incorrectly, and a site manager who is clearly hiding something. We are not going to start forcing doors open after dark.”

“Daniel is there.”

“We don’t know that.”

“He spoke to me.”

“Through a wall.”

“Yes.”

“Which means we need to work out what the wall is doing.”

Mara looked at the key.

Sana followed her gaze.

“Let me see.”

She held it up.

The metal was cold.

There was a small marking near the head.

Not a number.

A symbol.

Four short lines meeting at a point, with a fifth line extending beyond them.

Sana examined it.

“Looks like a survey mark.”

Mara shook her head.

“No.”

“What?”

“It’s not a survey mark.”

“How do you know?”

“Because I’ve seen it before.”

“Where?”

Mara looked towards the harbour.

“On Daniel’s drawings.”

Sana opened the car door.

“Get in.”

They drove to Mara’s hotel.

It was a small place near the station, with a reception desk and a lift that smelled of cleaning fluid. Mara had booked a room for three nights, assuming the survey would take two.

She had not expected to find her brother.

She had not expected to find a room that wasn’t on the plans.

She had not expected the past to be waiting behind a wall.

Sana sat on the edge of the bed while Mara opened her laptop.

“Show me the drawings.”

Mara connected to her cloud storage.

She had kept a folder of family documents for years. Most of it had been scanned from her mother’s house after the funeral: photographs, school reports, letters, the police file, Daniel’s old notebooks.

She found the folder.

Inside were twenty-three photographs of drawings.

She opened the first.

A corridor.

The second.

A staircase.

The third.

A door.

The fourth was a floor plan of the Meridian Hotel.

Mara zoomed in.

The drawing was crude but recognisable. Daniel had copied the outline from an old brochure. The rooms were numbered. The reception desk was marked. The kitchen, the stairs, the lift, the dining room.

But beside the north staircase, Daniel had drawn another corridor.

It ran parallel to the existing one.

There were five doors along it.

The first was marked 0A.

The second 0B.

The third 0C.

The fourth 0D.

The fifth 0E.

Mara opened another drawing.

The same corridor.

This time, there were six doors.

The final one was marked 0F.

Beneath it, in Daniel’s handwriting:

THE ROOM THAT IS BESIDE ALL THE OTHERS.

Sana leaned closer.

“That’s the symbol.”

Mara nodded.

At the top of the drawing, Daniel had written a date.

14 March 1997.

The same date as the first entry in the black notebook.

Sana looked at her.

“How old was he?”

“Fifteen.”

“That’s when he started drawing these?”

“Yes.”

“Did he ever tell you what they meant?”

Mara shook her head.

“He didn’t talk about them with me.”

“Why not?”

“Because I was younger.”

“By how much?”

“Three years.”

Sana looked at the drawings.

“That’s not much.”

“It was to him.”

Mara opened the final image.

It showed a room.

A square room with a table, a chair, and a window.

There were no doors.

In the corner, Daniel had written:

IF YOU CAN SEE THE WINDOW, SOMEONE CAN SEE YOU.

Sana read it aloud.

Then she looked at Mara.

“That’s the room Elsie described.”

“Yes.”

“How did he know?”

Mara stared at the drawing.

At the bottom was another line, written in a different hand.

The letters were smaller, more deliberate.

DON’T LOOK BACK WHEN IT SPEAKS.

Mara sat very still.

“That’s not Daniel’s handwriting,” she said.

Sana examined it.

“No.”

“Who wrote it?”

“I don’t know.”

Mara looked at the date.

There was no date beside the warning.

She turned to the next drawing.

It showed the hotel from above.

A second outline had been drawn around the first. The two shapes were almost identical, except that the second one was shifted slightly to the left.

A line connected them.

At the bottom, Daniel had written:

THE BUILDING DOESN’T MOVE. WE DO.

Sana looked up.

“What’s that supposed to mean?”

Mara did not answer.

She was thinking about the shadow.

The hotel shadow had fallen towards the sea.

Not because the sun had been wrong.

Not because the street had been angled.

Because the building had been occupying a position that the rest of the world could not account for.

She opened the photograph she had taken outside.

Then she enlarged the shadow.

At first, she saw nothing.

Then she noticed a detail.

The shadow did not end at the kerb.

It continued across the road, over the pavement, and through the wall of the building opposite.

It was not a shadow cast onto the street.

It was a shape passing through it.

Mara zoomed in further.

There was something inside the shadow.

A rectangle.

A door.

She looked at the photograph until her eyes hurt.

Then she turned the laptop towards Sana.

“Tell me what you see.”

Sana leaned closer.

She stared at the screen.

“That’s a door.”

“Yes.”

“In the shadow?”

“Yes.”

“Where does it go?”

Mara shook her head.

“I don’t know.”

Sana sat back.

For a while, neither spoke.

Then Sana said:

“We need to understand the geometry.”

Mara looked at her.

“What?”

“The drawings. The corridor. The second outline. They’re not random. Daniel was trying to describe a relationship between two spaces.”

“Two buildings?”

“Maybe. Or two versions of the same building.”

Mara shook her head.

“No. Elsie said the Between wasn’t above or below. It was beside.”

Sana looked at the screen.

“Then we need a word for beside.”

Mara thought about it.

In surveying, beside was easy.

It meant a location relative to another location. It could be measured, plotted, and recorded.

But Daniel had not meant beside in the ordinary sense.

He had meant a direction.

A direction that could not be represented on a normal plan.

She opened a blank document.

At the top, she typed:

THE MERIDIAN: SPATIAL ANOMALY INVESTIGATION

Then she paused.

She deleted the title.

Typed again.

THE BETWEEN

She stared at the words.

Then added:

Working hypothesis: the Meridian Hotel may occupy a spatial relationship with a second environment that is not represented in conventional three-dimensional survey data.

Sana read over her shoulder.

“That’s a very careful way of saying something impossible.”

“It’s not impossible.”

“How do you know?”

Mara looked at the drawings.

“Because if it were impossible, Daniel couldn’t have drawn it.”

Sana was quiet.

Then she said:

“Drawings aren’t proof.”

“No.”

“Photographs aren’t proof.”

“No.”

“Voices through walls aren’t proof.”

Mara looked at her.

“No.”

Sana folded her arms.

“Then what is?”

Mara looked at the key.

“Getting through the door.”

#### 7. The Wrong Direction

At 8:17 the following morning, Mara returned to the Meridian.

She had slept for less than two hours.

Sana had slept not at all.

They arrived with a laser distance meter, a digital camera, a roll of marking tape, two torches, a first-aid kit, a portable recorder, and a set of hand tools.

They also had a plan.

It was not a good plan.

It was simply the best one they could devise without knowing what they were dealing with.

The site gate was open.

Peter Hales was nowhere to be seen.

Mara unlocked the main entrance with the temporary key.

Inside, the lobby looked unchanged.

The photograph of the 1958 gathering remained on the counter.

Daniel still stood at the edge.

Mara walked towards it.

She lifted the frame.

On the back, the handwritten note had changed.

The original words remained:

Meridian summer gathering, August 1958. Staff and guests.

But beneath them, a new line had appeared.

Daniel, please stop standing at the edge.

And beneath that:

He can see you now.

Mara dropped the frame.

The glass struck the counter and cracked.

Sana caught it before it fell to the floor.

“What happened?”

Mara pointed.

Sana read the writing.

Her expression hardened.

“That’s enough.”

She took the frame and placed it face down.

“Whatever this is, we do not let it dictate what we do next.”

Mara nodded.

She was grateful for the words, though she did not know whether she believed them.

They went to the office.

The wall was still intact.

The wallpaper was still blue.

The black notebook remained on the desk.

Mara opened it.

The final entry had changed again.

ROOM 0F: OCCUPIED.

ONE PERSON OUTSIDE. ONE PERSON INSIDE.

Below it, in a new line:

TWO PEOPLE OUTSIDE. ONE PERSON INSIDE.

Sana looked at the page.

“That’s us.”

Mara nodded.

She took a photograph.

Then she opened Daniel’s drawings on the laptop and placed them beside the notebook.

The room numbers matched.

The symbol matched.

The dates matched.

She measured the wall again.

Four metres and twelve centimetres.

The plan said three metres and seventy.

She measured from the opposite corner.

The discrepancy remained.

Then she measured the room diagonally.

The laser returned a figure that made no sense.

She repeated the measurement.

Same result.

Sana checked the instrument.

“Calibration’s fine.”

“Measure it yourself.”

Sana took the device.

She stood where Mara had stood, aimed it at the far corner, and pressed the button.

The display showed a different number.

She tried again.

Different again.

“That’s not possible.”

Mara took the device.

She aimed it at the wall.

The measurement settled.

Then the display changed.

For a fraction of a second, a small symbol appeared beneath the distance reading.

Four lines meeting at a point.

A fifth line extending beyond them.

The same mark as the key.

Then the screen went blank.

Sana looked at Mara.

“Did you see that?”

“Yes.”

“Was it on the display?”

“Yes.”

“Not a reflection?”

“No.”

They stood in silence.

Then, from inside the wall, came three knocks.

Mara moved closer.

“Daniel?”

No answer.

Three more knocks.

Then a sound like a door being unlocked.

Mara looked at the key.

She walked towards the wall.

Sana caught her arm.

“Wait.”

Mara stopped.

“Listen.”

They heard a faint mechanical click.

Then another.

Then a third.

The wall seemed to vibrate.

A narrow vertical line appeared in the wallpaper.

It ran from the ceiling to the floor.

Mara took one step back.

The line widened.

A second line appeared, parallel to the first.

Then a third.

The wallpaper did not tear.

It moved.

The pattern shifted sideways, as though the wall itself were turning without changing its position.

A rectangle formed.

A door.

Not a door built into the wall.

A door where the wall had been.

It had no handle.

Only a small circular lock.

Mara took the key from her pocket.

Sana whispered:

“Don’t.”

Mara looked at her.

“If that’s him—”

“We don’t know who it is.”

“Then we’ll find out.”

“Not without thinking.”

Mara held the key.

The lock was exactly the same shape.

She stepped towards the door.

The room behind her seemed to grow quieter.

Even the distant traffic outside had vanished.

She placed the key in the lock.

It turned.

A soft click came from somewhere deep inside the building.

Then the door opened.

Beyond it was a narrow corridor.

The carpet was green.

The walls were pale.

A wooden chair stood at the far end.

Mara recognised the room immediately.

It was the room from Daniel’s drawing.

The room Elsie had described.

The room that should not exist.

Sana raised the camera.

“Wait.”

Mara looked back.

“What?”

“Look at the floor.”

Mara lowered her eyes.

The carpet inside the corridor was covered in footprints.

Some were old, pressed deep into the pile.

Some were fresh.

All led towards the chair.

None led back.

Mara looked up.

At the far end of the corridor, a door stood open.

A boy was standing in the doorway.

He was sixteen.

He was wearing a pale shirt.

His hair was dark.

His face was Daniel’s.

He looked at Mara.

Then he smiled.

Not the smile she remembered.

Not the quick, crooked grin he used to give her when he had stolen something from the kitchen.

This smile was slow.

Careful.

As though he had been practising it.

“Mara,” he said.

She stepped forward.

“Daniel.”

He looked over her shoulder.

At Sana.

Then back at Mara.

“You’re not alone.”

“No.”

He nodded.

“Good.”

Mara moved towards him.

“Come home.”

Daniel looked at the floor.

“I can’t.”

“Why?”

He glanced at the open door behind him.

“Because the house is still here.”

“What house?”

He looked at her.

“The one we left.”

Mara felt a coldness move through her.

“Daniel, what are you talking about?”

He took one step backwards.

The light behind him changed.

For an instant, the corridor seemed to stretch.

The chair at the far end became smaller.

The walls seemed to move apart.

Mara blinked.

The corridor was ordinary again.

Daniel raised one hand.

“Don’t come through yet.”

“Why?”

“Because you’re still looking at the door.”

Mara frowned.

“What?”

“You need to look beside it.”

She turned.

There was another door.

It was not visible from the angle she had been standing at.

It occupied the same section of wall as the first door, but a little to the left.

Its surface was black.

It had no lock.

No handle.

No frame.

Only the symbol.

Mara looked back at Daniel.

“What is that?”

He stopped smiling.

“That’s where it listens.”

A noise came from behind the black door.

Something moved.

Not footsteps.

Not a voice.

A pressure, like a large object being dragged across a floor that was not there.

Daniel’s expression changed.

“Mara, close it.”

She looked at him.

“Which one?”

“Both.”

“What?”

“Now.”

The sound grew louder.

Sana grabbed Mara’s shoulder.

“Back!”

The black door moved.

Not opened.

Moved.

Its outline shifted sideways across the wall, as though it were searching for a place to exist.

Daniel shouted:

“MARA!”

The green corridor flickered.

For one moment, Mara saw something beyond it.

A vast arrangement of rooms, all connected at impossible angles. Staircases that did not lead up or down. Windows opening onto other windows. Corridors that crossed through the same space without touching.

And people.

Hundreds of them.

Standing in doorways.

Looking towards her.

Then the view vanished.

The door slammed shut.

Mara was thrown backwards.

The office wall returned.

The wallpaper was flat.

The key was still in her hand.

Sana was on the floor.

The camera lay beside her.

For several seconds, neither moved.

Then Sana sat up.

“Are you hurt?”

Mara shook her head.

Sana looked at the wall.

“What was that?”

Mara stared at the place where the door had been.

“I don’t know.”

Sana stood, picked up the camera, and checked the display.

The recording had stopped at 08:43.

She played it back.

The footage showed the office.

The wall.

Mara standing in front of it.

Sana behind her.

Then a brief distortion.

Then nothing.

No door.

No corridor.

No Daniel.

Sana watched it twice.

“That’s not enough.”

Mara took the camera.

She played it again.

At the moment the distortion began, there was a single frame.

A fraction of a second.

A small white rectangle.

Inside it, a number.

0F.

Beneath the number was a time.

23:17.

Mara looked at the clock on her phone.

08:47.

“That’s not the time now,” Sana said.

“No.”

“Is it a date?”

“No.”

“What is it?”

Mara looked at the wall.

“It’s the time he disappeared.”

Sana frowned.

“How do you know?”

Mara swallowed.

“Because I was awake when it happened.”

She looked at the notebook.

The new line had appeared beneath the others.

TWO PEOPLE OUTSIDE. ONE PERSON INSIDE.

Then, below it:

THE LISTENER HAS NOTICED THE DOOR.

Mara closed the notebook.

She did not know what the Listener was.

She did not know whether Daniel was alive, or whether the person she had seen was a memory, a projection, a version of him, or something else entirely.

She did not know what the Between was.

But she knew one thing.

The door had opened.

And something had looked back.

## Chapter Three
### The Shape of a Missing Man

For a long time after the door closed, neither woman moved.

The office wall was ordinary again. Cream paint. A faint water stain near the ceiling. A radiator that had not worked since the hotel closed. The narrow crack where the skirting board had separated from the plaster.

Mara sat on the floor with her back against the opposite wall. Sana was beside her, one hand pressed to her mouth, the other gripping the crowbar.

The sandwiches lay scattered between them.

The clock on Sana’s phone showed 8:44 p.m.

Mara stared at it.

“That’s wrong,” she said.

Sana lowered her hand. “What is?”

“The time.”

“It’s eight forty-four.”

“It was eight forty-three when we opened the door.”

“Then a minute has passed.”

“No.”

Sana looked at the wall. “Mara.”

“The camera showed twenty-three seventeen. That’s the time Daniel disappeared. But the clock here—”

“Maybe the camera clock is wrong.”

“It isn’t.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I know because I checked it against the office clock before we opened the door.”

Sana looked towards the old analogue clock above the filing cabinet. Its hands were fixed at 11:17.

For a moment, neither of them spoke.

Then Sana stood.

“Right. We leave.”

Mara did not move.

“We leave,” Sana repeated. “We go somewhere with electricity, food and a working toilet. We do not open another door tonight.”

“He’s there.”

“Yes.”

“He’s alive.”

Sana’s expression changed. She looked down at Mara, and the practical argument she had been preparing seemed to leave her.

“I don’t know what he is,” she said gently. “I know what we saw. I know what you heard. But I don’t know what that means.”

“He knew me.”

“I heard him.”

“He remembered me.”

“I heard that too.”

“He told me to come home.”

Sana crouched in front of her.

“And he told you not to come through.”

Mara looked up.

“That’s important,” Sana said. “He didn’t say it because he was afraid of you. He said it because something was behind that other door. Something he thought could reach you.”

“The Listener.”

“Whatever that is.”

“It knows about us.”

“Yes.”

“It knows about the door.”

“Yes.”

“Then leaving doesn’t change anything.”

“It changes whether we’re standing in a condemned building at night with a mysterious hole in reality behind a wall.”

Mara almost laughed. The sound came out as a small, broken breath.

Sana put the crowbar down.

“We need to understand what we’re looking at,” she said. “And we need to do that before we go anywhere near it again.”

Mara looked at the wall.

On the floor beside her lay the strange key Elsie had given her. Its dark metal head caught the light from Sana’s phone. Four short lines met at a point. A fifth extended beyond them.

Mara picked it up.

It was warm.

Not warm from her hand. Warm as if someone had been holding it immediately before her.

She closed her fingers around it.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

“Tomorrow what?”

“We find out what happened to the others.”

Sana nodded slowly.

“Then we go.”

They left through the hotel’s main entrance. Outside, the harbour was black and the wind had come in from the east. The hotel stood behind them with its dark windows and its sealed upper floors. From the pavement, it looked smaller than it had from inside. Just a derelict building awaiting demolition.

Mara stopped at the gate.

There was something wrong with its shadow.

The streetlights cast long shadows towards the west. The hotel’s shadow stretched towards the harbour, where no light source could have put it. It was not quite a shadow. It was an absence in the wet pavement, a patch of darkness with an edge too straight to be natural.

At its far end, for the briefest moment, Mara saw a small rectangular shape.

A door.

She blinked.

It was gone.

“Come on,” Sana called.

Mara followed her.

Neither looked back.

---

The Bellwether Gazette occupied two rooms above a pharmacy on the High Street. Its archive was kept in a cupboard that smelled of damp cardboard and old glue. The current editor, a young man named Lewis, had never heard of the Meridian Hotel’s disappearance cases.

“People went missing?” he said.

“At different times,” Mara replied. “Over several decades.”

“That’s not something we’d bury.”

“Perhaps nobody thought there was a connection.”

Lewis looked at the dates on Sana’s handwritten list.

1958. 1971.		1983.		1997.	

There were more, but Mara had not yet been able to establish them.

“These aren’t all missing-person cases,” he said. “Some are accidents. Some are people who moved away.”

“Can we look?”

He considered them, then unlocked the archive cupboard.

“Don’t rearrange anything.”

They began with 1997.

The local paper had covered Daniel’s disappearance in three articles. The first was published on 24 November, the day after he vanished. It described a sixteen-year-old boy last seen near the Meridian Hotel. The second, two weeks later, reported that police had widened their search to the harbour. The third, published in January, said the investigation remained open.

There was a photograph.

Daniel stood outside the family home, one hand raised to shield his eyes from the sun. His hair was too long. He wore a school jumper with a frayed cuff. He was looking at the camera with an expression Mara remembered: not quite smiling, but already amused by whatever the photographer had said.

The caption read:

DANIEL VENN, 16, MISSING SINCE NOVEMBER 23.

Mara touched the newspaper.

“That’s him,” Sana said quietly.

“Yes.”

“He looks exactly like the boy in the doorway.”

“Yes.”

Sana studied the photograph.

“Twenty-nine years.”

“Not for him.”

They searched the preceding months. There were no reports of other disappearances at the hotel. No mention of a sealed stairwell. No mention of strange rooms or a woman hearing voices through walls.

But there was a short article from August 1997, buried on page six.

LOCAL HOTEL TO CLOSE FOR REFURBISHMENT

The Meridian, it said, was to undergo a programme of structural repairs. The work would include a new lift system, alterations to the north stairwell and the replacement of several internal walls.

The article named the building contractor.

Harker & Vale Construction.

Sana made a note.

“Your company?” she asked.

“Not mine.”

“I meant the name.”

“No relation.”

“Good. I’d hate to discover you’re part of a family conspiracy.”

Mara gave her a look.

Sana returned to the article.

“North stairwell alterations. That’s interesting.”

“Why?”

“Because the stairwell was sealed after the refurbishment, according to the maintenance records Hales showed us. If they changed the lift and the stairs at the same time, they may have altered the geometry of the building.”

“Buildings don’t have geometry that can be altered by accident.”

“All buildings have geometry. Most of it is hidden behind walls.”

She turned the page.

There was another article, dated December 1958.

MERIDIAN HOTEL CELEBRATES ITS FIFTIETH YEAR

The photograph was the same one in the hotel reception, though this version was grainy and cropped. Staff and guests stood in the lobby beneath a paper banner. A pianist sat at the edge of the frame.

Mara leaned closer.

There, near the far wall, stood Daniel.

Not a boy who looked like Daniel.

Daniel.

Sixteen years old. Same face. Same hair. Same expression.

The newspaper had printed the image nearly twenty-four years before he was born.

“That isn’t possible,” Sana whispered.

“No.”

“Could it be someone else?”

“No.”

“You’re certain?”

Mara pointed to the right hand.

Daniel had a small pale scar across the knuckle of his middle finger. He had got it opening a tin of peaches when he was nine. Mara had laughed at him for crying. He had thrown the tin at her and missed.

The scar was visible in the photograph.

Sana sat back.

“All right.”

“All right?”

“I believe you.”

Mara looked at her.

“You’re not going to tell me it’s a resemblance?”

“No. I am going to tell you that I don’t know what it means.”

They asked Lewis to make a copy.

He looked at the photograph, then at the two women.

“Is there something I’m missing?”

“Possibly,” Sana said.

Lewis scanned the picture.

The machine hummed. The image appeared on the screen. For several seconds, the scan was ordinary.

Then the photograph changed.

Not dramatically. No flash, no distortion, no sudden appearance of a face. The people remained where they were.

But the space behind Daniel became deeper.

The wall behind him appeared to extend away from the lobby, revealing a corridor that could not have fitted inside the hotel. Along that corridor were doors. Many doors. Some open, some closed.

And at the far end stood a man in a dark coat.

Mara felt the blood drain from her face.

The man was facing the scanner.

His features were indistinct, but his posture was unmistakable. He was looking directly at them.

The screen flickered.

The corridor vanished.

Lewis leaned towards the monitor.

“That’s odd.”

“What?” Sana asked.

“There’s a scratch on the scan.”

He pointed at a thin vertical line beside Daniel’s shoulder.

Mara stared at it.

The line was not a scratch.

It was the edge of a door.

“Can you print it?” she asked.

“Sure.”

Lewis sent the image to the printer.

The paper emerged warm.

Mara took it.

In the printed version, the corridor was gone. The man was gone. The only evidence of anything unusual was a faint vertical line behind Daniel.

But the line was not in the original photograph.

Sana folded the paper carefully and put it in her bag.

“That’s enough for today,” she said.

Mara was still looking at the screen.

“There’s something else.”

“What?”

“Look at Daniel.”

Sana turned back.

In the photograph, Daniel was not looking at the camera.

He was looking to the left, towards a place outside the frame.

And his expression was no longer amused.

He looked frightened.

---

They found the first useful record in the town’s old planning office.

It was a building alteration plan from 1958, submitted when the Meridian had been converted from a private residence into a hotel. The drawing showed five floors, a basement, two staircases and a lift shaft.

Sana laid it across the table.

“Here,” she said.

Mara leaned over.

The north stairwell was drawn in black ink. The lift shaft in red. The corridor ran from the reception area to the rear of the building.

“What’s wrong?” Mara asked.

Sana took a ruler from her bag.

“The north stairwell is too wide.”

“How much?”

“About forty centimetres.”

“That’s not unusual.”

“It is when the external walls and the lift shaft are both measured correctly.”

She moved the ruler.

“There should be a service cupboard here. But there’s no cupboard on the plan. And the corridor ends here.”

“Where does it go in the actual building?”

“It doesn’t.”

Sana pointed to the wall.

“That’s the office.”

Mara looked at the plan again.

The office had been added in the 1970s. Its walls were not where the original drawing said they should be. One of them was displaced by forty-two centimetres.

The same forty-two centimetres that had separated the office’s measured width from its listed dimensions.

“Someone built over the corridor,” Mara said.

“Maybe.”

“Or they built the office around it.”

Sana considered that.

“That’s worse.”

“Why?”

“Because it means they knew it was there.”

A voice spoke behind them.

“Not necessarily.”

They turned.

An old man stood in the doorway. He wore a brown coat and held a rolled drawing beneath one arm. His hair was white, his face narrow and deeply lined.

“Mr Rook?” Sana asked.

The man nodded.

“Elian Rook. I was told you wanted the old Meridian plans.”

Mara stood.

“Thank you for coming.”

“I knew your brother.”

The words landed heavily.

Mara stared at him.

“You knew Daniel?”

“Not well. He came to the planning office with his mother once. He was interested in the building.”

“Why?”

Elian unrolled the drawing.

“Because he believed the plans were incomplete.”

He placed it on the table.

It was not an architectural drawing. It was a map, though not of any place Mara recognised. The Meridian appeared as a simple rectangle, but beside it was another rectangle, offset slightly to the right. Between them ran a narrow strip marked with short parallel lines.

At intervals, small squares were drawn along the strip.

Doors.

Mara recognised the symbol.

The same four lines meeting at a point, with a fifth extending beyond them.

“Where did you get this?” she asked.

“From Daniel.”

“When?”

“November 1997. A few days before he disappeared.”

Mara looked at the map.

“Why didn’t you give it to the police?”

Elian’s expression changed.

“I did.”

“What happened?”

“They told me it was a child’s drawing.”

“It isn’t.”

“No.”

He rolled the map back up.

“Your brother asked me a question. He asked whether two rooms could occupy the same place without touching.”

Mara felt the room become very quiet.

“What did you say?”

“I said that depended on what you meant by the same place.”

“And what did he say?”

Elian looked at the table.

“He said, ‘I don’t mean the same place. I mean the place beside it.’”

Mara thought of Elsie Ward’s words.

Beside, not above or below.

“Did you understand him?” she asked.

“No.”

“Do you now?”

Elian looked at her.

“Not enough.”

He handed her the rolled drawing.

“There’s something else. Daniel came back to see me three weeks after he vanished.”

Mara’s fingers tightened around the paper.

“He came back?”

“Yes.”

“What did he look like?”

“Exactly as he had before.”

“Did he say where he’d been?”

Elian nodded.

“He said he’d found the other side of the wall.”

“And?”

“And he asked me to make a map.”

“Of what?”

Elian’s face seemed to grow older.

“Of the rooms that weren’t there.”

He looked at Mara.

“I didn’t.”

“Why not?”

“Because I saw something in the window behind him.”

“What?”

“A man standing in a corridor. Watching us.”

Mara thought of the man in the scanned photograph.

“Was he wearing a dark coat?”

Elian did not answer immediately.

Then he nodded.

“Yes.”

“Did Daniel see him?”

“He did.”

“What did he say?”

Elian’s voice dropped.

“He said, ‘Don’t let it learn your name.’”

## Chapter Four
### The Caretaker’s Instructions

Peter Hales was waiting at the hotel the following morning.

He stood beside the reception desk with a clipboard and a face that suggested he had spent the night arguing with himself.

“You shouldn’t have been in the building after hours,” he said.

Mara took off her coat.

“Good morning to you too.”

“I’ve had a call from the security company. They say the alarm was triggered.”

“Was it?”

“At 8:43.”

Sana looked at Mara.

Hales noticed.

“What happened?”

“Nothing,” Mara said.

He studied her for a moment.

“You opened something.”

“We found an inconsistency in the office wall.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

Mara took the key from her pocket and placed it on the reception desk.

Hales went pale.

“Where did you get that?”

“Elsie Ward.”

He stepped back.

“She shouldn’t have given it to you.”

“She was keeping it for me.”

“She shouldn’t have.”

“Why?”

“Because it isn’t a key.”

Mara looked at it.

“It opened a door.”

“Not that door.”

Hales glanced towards the office corridor.

For a moment, he seemed to be listening.

Then he lowered his voice.

“Come with me.”

He led them through the service passage and into a small room behind the kitchen. The walls were lined with shelves, old cleaning equipment and boxes of replacement fittings. A single fluorescent tube buzzed overhead.

Hales shut the door.

“I’ve worked here ten years,” he said. “I have seen things I can’t explain. I’ve heard people speaking in rooms that were empty. I’ve found wet footprints on floors where the windows were locked. I’ve had the lift stop between floors with nobody inside it.”

“Why didn’t you report it?” Sana asked.

“To whom? The police? The council? The people who own the building? I’ve reported every fault I could put on a form. The rest I learned to leave alone.”

Mara placed the key on a shelf.

“What happened to Daniel?”

Hales looked at her.

“I don’t know.”

“That’s not true.”

“I know what people said.”

“What did they say?”

He rubbed his face.

“That he went up the north stairwell. That he came back. That he disappeared again. That he was seen in the lobby after he was already missing. That the lift carried him to floors that don’t exist.”

“Did you see him?”

Hales nodded.

“When?”

“Three weeks after he vanished.”

Mara felt the old ache open inside her.

“Where?”

“Here. In the lobby. He was standing by the desk. I was younger then. I was doing maintenance work. I asked him if he was lost.”

“What did he say?”

“He asked if the night receptionist was still here.”

“Elsie.”

“Yes. I told him she was retired. He looked disappointed. Then he asked me whether the hotel was going to be demolished.”

Mara swallowed.

“What did you say?”

“I said no. It had only just been refurbished.”

“And?”

“He said, ‘It will be. They always knock it down when they stop believing in it.’”

Sana exchanged a glance with Mara.

“Did he look different?” she asked.

“No. Sixteen. Same clothes. Same hair. Same face.”

“Did he seem frightened?”

Hales thought about it.

“No. He seemed tired.”

“Was he alone?”

Hales shook his head.

“Not exactly.”

“What does that mean?”

“I saw his reflection in the glass behind reception. There was someone standing beside him.”

“Who?”

“I don’t know. The reflection was wrong. It looked like a person standing too close to him, but there wasn’t anyone there.”

He took a breath.

“Then the lights went out. When they came back, Daniel was gone.”

Mara stared at him.

“Why didn’t you tell anyone?”

“I did. I told the police. They said I was confused.”

“Did you tell my mother?”

Hales looked away.

“She came here herself.”

“When?”

“After Daniel disappeared. She asked me to let her into the north stairwell.”

Mara felt the room tilt.

“Did you?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because I’d seen what happened to Daniel.”

“She went in anyway.”

Hales looked at her sharply.

“How do you know that?”

“Elsie told me.”

He closed his eyes.

“Of course she did.”

“Did you know she saw herself in the lobby?”

Hales did not answer.

Mara moved closer.

“Peter. What happened to my mother?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did she come back?”

“Yes.”

“Was she different?”

He looked at the door.

“She asked me to promise something.”

“What?”

“That if the hotel ever developed another door, I would make sure nobody opened it.”

Mara felt cold.

“Why didn’t you?”

Hales gave a bitter laugh.

“Because I didn’t know how.”

He took a folded sheet of paper from his clipboard.

It was a list of instructions, typed on an old typewriter. The paper had yellowed at the edges.

“I found this in the maintenance room when I took over the site,” he said. “It was pinned behind the electrical panel. I thought it was some kind of joke.”

He placed it on the shelf.

At the top, in capital letters, were the words:

MERIDIAN HOTEL — NIGHT PROCEDURE

Below them were six instructions.

1. If the lift stops between floors, do not open the doors.
2. If a guest asks for Room 0F, say that the hotel has no such room.
3. If the north stairwell is open, do not enter it alone.
4. If you hear someone calling from behind a wall, do not answer.
5. If a person appears in the lobby whom you know to be missing, do not approach them.
6. If the bell rings at the reception desk and nobody is present, do not look at the mirror.

Mara read the list twice.

“Who wrote this?”

“I don’t know.”

“How old is it?”

“Could be from the fifties. Could be older.”

“Why keep it?”

“Because it was easier than throwing it away.”

Sana leaned towards the paper.

“There’s a seventh instruction.”

Hales looked at her.

She pointed to the bottom of the page.

There was a faint impression, as if another sheet had once been pressed against it while someone wrote.

Sana angled the paper towards the fluorescent light.

The indentation became visible.

7. IF THE LISTENER SPEAKS, DO NOT GIVE IT A NAME.

Mara read it aloud.

The room seemed to contract around the words.

“What is the Listener?” she asked.

Hales shook his head.

“I don’t know.”

“Have you heard it?”

“Yes.”

“What does it sound like?”

He hesitated.

“Like someone you know, speaking from the next room.”

Mara thought of Daniel’s voice through the wall.

You’re late.

“Has it ever spoken to you?”

Hales looked at her.

“Once.”

“What did it say?”

“It asked me where the caretaker had gone.”

“Who was the caretaker?”

Hales looked towards the ceiling.

“I think it meant me.”

---

They found the caretaker’s room at the end of the ground-floor service corridor.

It was not on the plans.

The door was painted the same cream as the walls. There was no handle, only a keyhole, narrow and vertical. Mara tried the strange key.

It turned without resistance.

The door opened inwards.

The room beyond was small, perhaps three metres square. A narrow bed stood against one wall. A washbasin occupied the corner. There was a desk, a chair and a wardrobe with one door hanging loose.

The room was clean.

Not merely tidy. Clean, as if someone had swept it that morning.

A mug stood on the desk beside a closed book.

Mara stepped inside.

The air smelled of soap and cold stone.

“Someone’s been here,” Sana said.

Hales remained in the doorway.

“Nobody has access to this room.”

“Then who cleaned it?” Sana asked.

“I don’t know.”

Mara picked up the book.

It was a ledger, bound in dark blue cloth. Its pages were filled with names and dates. Some entries were written in different hands, but the handwriting was remarkably consistent in one respect: every name had been crossed out with a single line.

The earliest entry was dated 1908.

The most recent was dated 23 November 2016.

Mara turned the pages.

There were dozens of names.

Some she recognised from the old newspaper. Some were unfamiliar. A few had notes beside them.

MISSING — 1912.

RETURNED — 1912.

NOT THE SAME PERSON.

SEEN IN LOBBY — 1958.

DO NOT FOLLOW.

Then, on a page near the back, she found:

DANIEL VENN — 23 NOVEMBER 1997.

Below it:

RETURNED — 14 DECEMBER 1997.

And beneath that:

NOT RELEASED.

Mara gripped the edge of the desk.

“He came back.”

Sana read the entry over her shoulder.

“He came back, but they didn’t let him go.”

“Who didn’t?”

Neither woman answered.

Mara turned another page.

There was an entry in the same handwriting as the note beneath the basement door.

MARA VENN — 23 NOVEMBER 2016.

Beside it, someone had written:

OUTSIDE.

She looked at Sana.

“There’s another name.”

Sana followed her gaze.

SANA IDRIS — 23 NOVEMBER 2016.

Beside it:

OUTSIDE.

Mara turned the page.

The next entry had no date.

THE LISTENER — STATUS: AWARE.

Below it:

IT HAS LEARNED THE SHAPE OF THE DOOR.

Sana closed the ledger.

“We’re leaving.”

Mara did not move.

“Now.”

“There’s more.”

“There will always be more.”

Mara turned towards the wardrobe.

Its door was slightly open.

Inside hung a dark coat.

The coat was old-fashioned, long and black, with a narrow collar. It looked exactly like the coat worn by the figure in the scanned photograph.

On the inside of the collar was a small white label.

MERIDIAN HOTEL — CARETAKER.

Mara stepped back.

The coat shifted.

Not from a draught. The room was still.

Its sleeve lifted slowly, as if an unseen arm were reaching out.

Then a voice came from inside the wardrobe.

“Miss Venn.”

It was not Daniel’s voice.

It was a man’s voice, calm and polite, with the faintest trace of amusement.

Mara could not move.

“Miss Venn,” it said again. “You’ve brought a friend.”

Sana seized Mara’s arm.

“Don’t answer.”

The voice continued.

“How very considerate.”

The wardrobe door swung shut.

Hales made a small sound in the doorway.

“Get out,” he whispered.

Mara backed away.

The key was still in the lock.

As she reached for it, something knocked from inside the wardrobe.

Once.

Twice.

Then three times.

The same rhythm as the reception bell.

Hales pulled the door shut behind them.

“Never go in there again,” he said.

“What is it?” Mara demanded.

He looked at her with an expression she had not seen before.

Not fear.

Pity.

“It’s where the caretaker keeps the things that don’t belong to the hotel.”

## Chapter Five
### What the Camera Saw

Sana insisted they spend the afternoon somewhere ordinary.

They went to a café overlooking the harbour, where a child dropped a spoon and cried, a man argued with his wife about parking, and the espresso machine made a noise like a small aircraft failing to start.

Mara watched all of it with the attention of someone who had forgotten how the world worked.

She had the ledger’s entries written in her notebook.

Daniel Venn — returned, not released.

Mara Venn — outside.

Sana Idris — outside.

The Listener — aware.

“What does ‘not released’ mean?” Sana asked.

Mara looked up.

“I don’t know.”

“Could mean he came back but didn’t leave the building.”

“He appeared in the lobby.”

“Could have been a reflection.”

“Elian saw him.”

“Elian saw someone.”

“Peter saw him.”

“Peter saw someone too.”

Mara put down her cup.

“You’re trying to make this ordinary.”

“I’m trying to keep us from deciding we understand it when we don’t.”

Mara looked out at the harbour.

The tide was low. Mudflats stretched between the stone piers, and the old ferry terminal stood empty on the opposite shore.

“Daniel came back,” she said. “He was here. He was sixteen. He was in the lobby, and nobody took him home.”

Sana said nothing.

“He was right there.”

“I know.”

“And I was twelve. I was at home. I was waiting.”

Sana reached across the table and covered Mara’s hand.

“You were a child.”

“I should have looked for him.”

“You did.”

“Not there.”

“You couldn’t have known.”

Mara pulled her hand away.

“I knew he was in that building. I knew it.”

Sana did not argue.

For a while they sat in silence.

Then Sana opened her bag and took out the photograph from the Gazette.

“We need to look at this properly.”

“There’s nothing to see.”

“That’s what I thought.”

She placed the print on the table.

Daniel stood at the edge of the 1958 gathering. Behind him, the faint line marked the edge of the impossible corridor.

Sana took out a small magnifying glass.

“Look at the glass behind him.”

Mara leaned forward.

The reception desk had a large mirror behind it. In the original photograph, it reflected the lobby, the staircase and several people standing near the entrance.

In the printed copy, the mirror reflected something else.

A corridor.

And in the corridor stood the man in the dark coat.

He was not looking at Daniel.

He was looking at the camera.

“He’s looking at us,” Mara said.

Sana nodded.

“Not at the photographer. At the camera now.”

“That’s impossible.”

“Yes.”

Sana took out her phone and photographed the print.

The image appeared on the screen.

The mirror was blank.

She took another photograph.

This time the man appeared, but he was closer.

Sana lowered the phone.

“All right.”

Mara felt the old fear begin to rise.

“Put it away.”

Sana did.

They left the café and walked to the car park.

On the way, Mara noticed a shop window reflecting the street. She saw herself and Sana walking past, their coats moving in the wind.

Then she saw a third figure between them.

A tall man in a dark coat.

She turned.

There was nobody there.

In the window, the man remained.

His head was slightly tilted.

Mara kept walking.

“Don’t look back,” she said.

Sana glanced at her.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

But the reflection followed them all the way to the car.

The security system at the Meridian had been upgraded in 2006. It used a mixture of fixed cameras, motion sensors and an old digital recorder in the basement office. Hales had shown them the system on their first day, but the archive was incomplete. Some recordings had been overwritten, others had been lost when the building’s network failed.

Sana knew how to retrieve the remaining files.

They returned to the hotel just before dusk.

Hales met them at the entrance.

“I told you not to go into that room again.”

“We won’t,” Sana said.

“Then what are you doing here?”

“Looking at the cameras.”

He shook his head.

“There’s nothing on them.”

“That’s what we’re checking.”

He let them in, but followed them to the basement office.

The security room was narrow and windowless. Six monitors were mounted on the wall. A rack of recording equipment stood beneath them, its fans whining.

Sana connected her laptop.

“Do you have the original recordings from November 1997?” she asked.

Hales looked at her.

“Why?”

“Because we want to see the night Daniel disappeared.”

“They won’t be there.”

“Why not?”

“Old system.”

“Was there a system?”

“Yes.”

“Then there may be tapes.”

Hales rubbed his forehead.

“I don’t know.”

Mara looked at him.

“Peter.”

He sighed.

“There’s a box in the basement store. Old tapes. I never checked them.”

He unlocked a cupboard and pulled out a cardboard box.

Inside were VHS cassettes, each labelled with a date and camera number.

Sana began sorting them.

“November 1997,” she said. “Here.”

The tape was labelled:

23/11/97 — LOBBY — CAM 01.

They loaded it into an old player connected to the recorder.

The image appeared.

The lobby was empty.

The clock above reception showed 11:06 p.m.

A cleaner crossed the frame, carrying a bucket. She disappeared down the service corridor. A few minutes later, the lift doors opened. Nobody emerged.

The clock moved to 11:12.

At 11:16, the lobby lights flickered.

Mara leaned forward.

At 11:17, the north stairwell door opened.

A boy stepped out.

Daniel.

He was wearing the same school jumper from the newspaper photograph. His hair was damp. His face was pale.

He looked around the lobby.

Then he looked directly at the camera.

Mara’s breath caught.

“That’s him.”

Sana froze the image.

Daniel’s face filled the monitor.

He looked older than sixteen for one brief instant. Not in his features, but in his expression. He looked like someone who had been away for a very long time.

Sana pressed play.

Daniel walked towards reception.

He stopped.

The bell on the desk rang once.

Nobody had touched it.

Daniel turned towards the mirror.

A figure appeared in the reflection.

The man in the dark coat.

Daniel did not seem surprised.

He said something.

The recording had no sound.

The man in the reflection raised one hand.

Daniel shook his head.

Then he turned and walked towards the lift.

The doors opened before he reached them.

He stepped inside.

The doors closed.

The lift did not move.

The clock showed 11:18.

Then, slowly, the reflection in the mirror changed.

The man in the dark coat remained.

But now he was standing where Daniel had been.

Facing the camera.

Sana stopped the tape.

“That’s enough.”

Mara was staring at the screen.

“Play it again.”

“No.”

“Please.”

Sana hesitated, then rewound.

They watched again.

This time Mara paid attention to Daniel’s mouth.

He had spoken three words.

She slowed the recording.

Frame by frame, the movement became clear.

DON’T LET IT IN.

Mara felt the words settle inside her.

“He’s warning us.”

“Yes,” Sana said.

“He’s been warning us for nineteen years.”

Sana did not answer.

Mara looked at the monitor.

“Play the next tape.”

They searched the box.

There were recordings from November 1997, December 1997 and January 1998. The next tape was labelled:

14/12/97 — LOBBY — CAM 01.

The date Daniel had returned.

Sana inserted it.

The lobby appeared again.

The clock showed 9:02 p.m.

At 9:17, the lift doors opened.

Daniel stepped out.

He looked exactly as he had three weeks earlier.

Sixteen years old.

He carried a small brown paper parcel.

He walked to reception and placed the parcel on the desk.

Then he looked up at the camera.

His lips moved.

Sana slowed the image.

Daniel said:

SHE MUST NOT COME.

Mara stared.

“Me.”

Sana nodded.

Daniel turned towards the mirror.

The man in the dark coat appeared in the reflection.

This time, he was standing much closer.

Daniel picked up the parcel and walked towards the north stairwell.

He stopped at the door.

For a moment, he looked directly at the camera again.

Then he held up his right hand.

Between his fingers was a small key.

The same key Mara had received from Elsie.

He placed it on the reception desk.

The tape flickered.

The lobby disappeared.

For three seconds, the screen showed a room.

A square room with a table, a chair and a window.

The room from Daniel’s drawing.

A young woman stood beside the window.

Mara’s mother.

She was looking out at the lobby.

On the other side of the glass, a second version of her stood in the corridor.

The second woman smiled.

Mara’s mother did not.

Then the image changed.

The room was empty.

On the wall, written in black letters, were the words:

THE FIRST ONE WAS NOT YOUR MOTHER.

Mara stopped breathing.

Sana turned towards her.

“What does that mean?”

Mara couldn’t speak.

The tape continued.

The lobby returned.

Daniel was gone.

The key remained on the desk.

And behind reception, the mirror reflected a corridor that stretched far beyond the dimensions of the hotel.

At its far end, the man in the dark coat was waiting.

He lifted his hand.

And the camera moved.

Not physically. The image shifted sideways, as if the camera had turned its head.

The man smiled.

Then the recording ended.

The monitor went black.

“Turn it off,” Hales whispered.

Sana pressed the stop button.

The room was silent except for the fans in the equipment rack.

Mara stood.

“The first one wasn’t my mother.”

“Maybe it’s referring to the reflection,” Sana said.

“No.”

“Or the version she saw.”

“No. It says the first one.”

Hales backed away from the monitors.

“I told you not to look.”

“Who is the first one?” Mara demanded.

“I don’t know.”

“You’ve seen this before.”

“I’ve seen parts of it.”

“What happened to my mother?”

Hales looked at her.

“She came here three times.”

“Three?”

“The first time, she went into the stairwell. The second time, she came back with a cut on her face. The third time, she told me she had found the room where Daniel was waiting.”

Mara felt the room go cold.

“Where?”

Hales shook his head.

“She didn’t say.”

“What did she do?”

“She asked me to keep the hotel closed.”

“It was open then.”

“Not for long.”

“Why?”

“Because she said the building was beginning to remember her.”

Mara stared at him.

“Remember her?”

Hales nodded.

“She said the rooms were changing when she walked through them. Doors appearing where there had been walls. Windows showing places she’d never been. She said the hotel was trying to make a copy of her.”

Sana looked at the frozen screen.

“Did she succeed?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did she find Daniel?”

Hales looked at Mara.

“She said she did.”

Mara’s throat tightened.

“What did he say?”

Hales swallowed.

“He said he was sorry.”

“For what?”

“I don’t know.”

Mara took the key from her pocket.

It was warm again.

On the monitor, the black screen showed a faint reflection of the room.

Mara could see herself.

Sana.

Hales.

And a fourth figure standing behind them.

A boy.

Sixteen years old.

Daniel.

He raised one finger to his lips.

Then he pointed at the monitor.

Mara turned.

There was nobody behind them.

When she looked back, the reflection had changed.

The boy was gone.

In his place stood the man in the dark coat.

His face was now clear.

It was Peter Hales.

Not the Peter Hales standing beside them.

A younger version of him, perhaps twenty-five years old, with the same narrow eyes and the same small scar beneath his chin.

The reflection smiled.

The real Hales made a choking sound.

“That’s not me,” he said.

The reflected man lifted a hand and pressed it against the inside of the screen.

The glass bowed.

Sana grabbed Mara’s arm.

“Move.”

The screen cracked.

A thin black line appeared from top to bottom.

From somewhere inside the equipment rack came the sound of a bell.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Then a voice spoke from the dark monitor.

“Peter.”

Hales stumbled backwards.

The voice was his own.

“Peter, open the door.”

The real Hales shook his head.

“No.”

“Peter.”

“No.”

“You’re the caretaker.”

The screen bowed further.

“You’re supposed to let us in.”

Mara seized the strange key.

The metal was burning hot now.

She pulled it from her pocket and held it towards the monitor.

The reflected man stopped smiling.

For the first time, his expression changed.

He looked afraid.

The screen snapped back into place.

The bell fell silent.

And somewhere deep in the building, the lift began to move.

Sana stared at the monitor.

“What did you just do?”

Mara looked at the key.

“I don’t know.”

The lift continued upwards.

One floor.

Then another.

Then another.

It passed the fifth floor.

There was no sixth floor.

The lift stopped.

A soft chime sounded through the hotel.

The doors opened.

From the corridor outside came a voice.

A boy’s voice.

“Mara?”

She looked towards the door.

Sana gripped her wrist.

“Don’t.”

The voice came again.

“Mara, it’s me.”

She knew that voice.

She had known it since childhood. It had called her in from the garden, shouted through the bathroom door, whispered jokes in the dark when their mother was asleep.

It was Daniel.

But this time, he did not sound sixteen.

He sounded exactly as he would have sounded now, if the years had passed normally.

Thirty-five.

Tired.

Older.

“Please,” he said. “I can’t hold it much longer.”

Mara took one step towards the door.

Sana held on.

“That’s not him,” she whispered.

The voice came again.

“Don’t let them knock it down.”

Then, from somewhere beyond the lift, another voice spoke.

It was the man in the dark coat.

It was quiet, courteous, and very close.

“Thank you, Peter.”

The lift doors began to close.

“Thank you for keeping the door.”

The lights went out.

And in the darkness, Mara heard the unmistakable sound of someone turning a key in a lock.
